Customer Complaints Analysis

Ten years of banking complaint records analysed end to end: profiled and cleaned in SQL, modelled into six service-quality KPIs, then delivered as an interactive dashboard where every chart filters every other. The question isn’t how many complaints did we get — it’s which products cause them, whether we resolve them well, and what changed over ten years.

Overview

Complaints are usually reported as a single number: how many arrived this month. That tells an operations manager almost nothing. It doesn’t say which products generate them, whether they are being resolved well, where they cluster geographically, or whether the trend is improving.

I took a decade of complaint records and worked them properly — profiling, cleaning, defining the measures, then building a report where every element is a filter. Click a product, a state or a year and all six KPIs plus the underlying records recalculate against that selection, so a question like “why did disputes spike in Victoria in 2018?” is two clicks rather than a new report request.

The questions

  • Where do complaints come from? Which products, issue types, intake channels and states generate the volume — and is it concentrated or spread?
  • Are we resolving them well? Timeliness, dispute rate, closure rate and whether the customer actually received relief.
  • Is the picture improving? How volume and resolution quality moved across ten years, and whether any year broke the pattern.
  • Can a non-analyst answer their own follow-up? The report had to let an operations lead drill into their own product or state without coming back to me.

The data

467 complaint records dated 26 August 2011 to 7 December 2020. Each row carries the product and sub-product, issue type, intake channel, state and postcode, the company’s response, whether it was answered on time, and whether the consumer disputed the outcome.

Before any measure was defined I profiled every column — row counts, distinct values, null rates, date ranges and outliers — because a KPI built on an unprofiled column is a guess with a decimal point on it.

How I built it

  • Profiled the raw extract first. Counted rows, distinct values, nulls and date ranges per column in SQL, and checked the date span for gaps. This is what surfaced the inconsistent category labels and the open cases that would later have skewed every rate.
  • Cleaned and standardised the categories. Product, sub-product, issue, channel, state and response values all arrived with variant spellings and casing. I mapped each to a single canonical label with lookup tables, so one category couldn’t silently split into three across the report.
  • Derived the analytical fields. Added year and month columns off the received date for trend work, a binary relief flag (monetary or non-monetary — explicitly excluding “closed without relief”, which is the easiest way to overstate the number), a digital-intake flag for web and email, and an open/closed status.
  • Defined the six KPIs and wrote them down. Total volume, timely response rate, consumer disputed rate, relief provided rate, case closure rate and digital intake share. Each one got a written definition — including the decision to count in-progress cases in volume but exclude them from the closed count — so the same number means the same thing every time it’s quoted.
  • Aggregated along every dimension that mattered. Grouped by product, issue, channel, state and year, and cross-tabbed the resolution measures against each — which is where the interesting result sits, because a high dispute rate only means something once you know which product it belongs to.
  • Validated before visualising. Reconciled every aggregate back to the 467-row source, checked that each breakdown summed to the total, and confirmed the rates against hand calculations on a sample. Nothing went on the dashboard that I couldn’t trace back to a row.
  • Designed the report around the questions, not the data. KPIs across the top for the headline read, then intake channel, trend and geography beneath. Every chart doubles as a filter, and each has a plain data table underneath it — because someone always wants the figures, not the picture.

What it shows

01

Half of all complaints now arrive digitally

Web and email make up just over 50% of intake, with referrals a distant second at 30%. That concentration is what makes digital channel monitoring worth resourcing — it is where the volume actually is.

02

Fast responses, but one in five still dispute

94.4% of complaints were answered on time, yet 20.6% of customers disputed the outcome anyway. Timeliness and resolution quality are measuring two different things, and only one of them was being hit.

03

Only a quarter of cases end in relief

24.2% closed with monetary or non-monetary relief; the majority closed with an explanation only. Read alongside the dispute rate, that is the most likely driver of customers pushing back.

04

Credit cards and mortgages lead the named products

Credit cards account for 25.5% of complaints and mortgages 16.5% — the two products where a process fix would remove the most volume.

05

Volume is geographically concentrated and peaked in 2018

NSW (31.0%) and Victoria (25.9%) together generate over half of all complaints, and 2018 was the high point of the ten-year series at 71 before volume eased back.

Outcome

The analysis turned a flat list of 467 rows into six defined, reconciled KPIs and a report that answers follow-up questions on its own. The clearest finding was the gap between responding and resolving: a 94% timeliness figure looks like a service success until it sits next to a 21% dispute rate and a 24% relief rate on the same screen.

The part I’d defend hardest is the least visible — the profiling, the category standardisation and the written KPI definitions. That work is what lets someone challenge a number in a meeting and get a traceable answer instead of a shrug.
